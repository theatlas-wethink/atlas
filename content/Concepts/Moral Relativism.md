
# Moral Relativism

**Moral Relativism** is the meta-ethical position that moral judgments, truths, and values are not objective, absolute, or universal. Instead, moral claims are relative to the social, cultural, historical, or individual contexts in which they are made.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Non-Objectivism**: Moral facts do not exist independently of human belief, culture, or framework.
2. **Context-Dependence**: The truth value of a moral statement ("$X$ is wrong") can vary depending on who makes it, when it is made, or the culture evaluating it.
3. **Absence of a Universal Standard**: There is no objective, archimedean point or overarching framework from which to rank one cultural morality as superior to another.

---

## Primary Variants & Examples

### 1. Cultural Relativism (Descriptive & Normative)
Assesses morality through the lens of societal norms and cultural heritage.
* **Descriptive Relativism:** Simply observes that different cultures maintain vastly different moral codes across history (e.g., historical perspectives on public executions, funerary practices, or economic systems).
* **Normative Relativism:** Asserts that one *ought not* judge or interfere with the practices of another culture, as moral truth is bounded by cultural boundaries.
* **Example:** In Herodotus's classic account, the ancient Greeks (who burned their dead parents) and the Callatians (who ate them) were both horrified by each other’s customs, yet each acted in accord with their own societal definition of filial piety.

### 2. Subjectivism (Individual Relativism)
Reduces moral claims entirely to personal preferences, emotional attitudes, or individual stances.
* *Mechanism:* Saying "Stealing is wrong" is functionally equivalent to saying "I personally dislike stealing."
* **Example:** Two individuals facing an ethical dilemma regarding resource distribution reach opposite conclusions. Under strict individual subjectivism, neither stance is objectively "correct"—they are simply reflections of differing internal axioms.

---

## Structural Critiques & Paradoxes

* **The Reformer's Dilemma:** If moral truth is strictly defined by cultural consensus, then moral reformers (like abolitionists or civil rights leaders) who challenge existing norms are, by definition, "immoral" within their society's current framework.
* **The Tolerance Paradox:** If normative cultural relativism claims that all cultural values are valid, it cannot consistently assert that "tolerance" is a universal duty without contradicting its own core axiom.
* **Inability to Condemn Extremes:** Under pure cultural relativism, external critique of state-sanctioned atrocities or institutionalized oppression in another culture lacks grounding beyond preference.

---

## Structural Counter-Frameworks

* [[Moral Absolutism]]: Holds that certain actions are intrinsically right or wrong regardless of context or culture.
* [[Utilitarianism]]: Evaluates morality objectively based on aggregate outcome calculations (utility maximization) rather than cultural norms.
* [[Virtue ethics]]: Derives universal moral duties from rational consistency rather than social consensus.


# Moral Absolutism

**Moral Absolutism** is an ethical position asserting that certain actions are intrinsically right or wrong, regardless of their consequences, context, or the intentions behind them. Under this framework, moral rules are universal, objective, and exceptionless.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Intrinsic Value of Actions**: The moral quality of an act resides within the act itself, rather than in its downstream effects.
2. **Universality**: Moral rules apply equally to all moral agents across all time periods, cultures, and situations.
3. **Non-Consequentialism**: Outcomes—no matter how favorable or catastrophic—cannot alter the moral status of a prohibited or mandated act.
4. **Objective Realism**: Moral facts exist independently of human belief, subjective preference, or social consensus.

---

## Key Frameworks & Examples

### 1. Deontological Absolutism (Kantian Ethics)
Immanuel Kant argued that moral duties are derived from reason and expressed through the *Categorical Imperative*. If an action cannot be consistently universalized without contradiction, it is strictly forbidden in every scenario.
* **The Murderer at the Door Paradox:** If a murderer asks for the location of your friend hiding in your house, Kantian absolutism dictates that lying is strictly immoral, even if telling the truth leads directly to your friend's death. The duty to refrain from lying is absolute and unconditional.

### 2. Religious / Divine Command Theory
Holds that actions are objectively right or wrong because a divine authority commands or forbids them.
* **Example (Divine Decrees):** Prohibitions against theft, murder, or bearing false witness are treated as absolute moral laws that bind human behavior regardless of societal utility or individual hardship.

### 3. Natural Rights Theory
Posits that human beings possess fundamental rights (such as bodily autonomy or the right to life) that no state, group, or outcome can legitimately violate.
* **Example (Torture Prohibitions):** Under an absolute rights framework, torturing an individual is categorically wrong—even in a "ticking time bomb" scenario where doing so might yield information that saves thousands of lives.

---

## Structural Conflicts & Edge Cases

* **The Conflict of Absolutes:** When two absolute rules collide (e.g., the duty to tell the truth vs. the duty to preserve human life), strict moral absolutism struggles to rank or resolve the conflict without breaking one of its own core axioms.
* **Moral Rigidity:** By ignoring context and consequences entirely, moral absolutism can produce outcomes that feel intuitively cruel or counterproductive.

---

## Structural Counter-Frameworks

* [[Utilitarianism]]: Holds that actions have no intrinsic moral value and are judged entirely by their aggregate consequences.
* [[Moral Relativism]]: Asserts that moral claims are relative to culture, context, or historical epoch rather than universal.
* [[Virtue ethics]]: Centers morality on the development of practical wisdom and character traits rather than rigid adherence to absolute rules.
